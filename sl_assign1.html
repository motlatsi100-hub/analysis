introduction

This study focuses on modelling and predicting the median value of owner-occupied homes (measured in $1000s) across suburbs in Boston using a well-known dataset consisting of 506 observations and 12 explanatory variables. These variables capture a range of socioeconomic, environmental, and structural characteristics that may influence housing prices.

The primary objective of this analysis is to develop and compare multiple statistical models to accurately predict housing values while identifying the most influential predictors. To achieve this, a structured data analysis pipeline was followed, beginning with Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA). This stage involved assessing the dataset’s structure, confirming the number and types of variables, and checking for missing values to ensure data quality. Various visualization techniques, including histograms, box plots, and scatter plots, were employed to understand the distribution of variables and their relationships with the target variable. Additionally, correlation analysis was conducted to quantify the strength and direction of linear relationships between predictors and housing prices.

Following the EDA, the dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets to enable robust model evaluation and to avoid overfitting. Several predictive modelling approaches were then implemented. A baseline model using multiple linear regression was first established, providing a foundation for comparison. Model selection techniques based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were applied to identify more parsimonious models that balance goodness-of-fit with model complexity. Furthermore, regularization methods, specifically Ridge and LASSO regression, were employed to address potential multicollinearity and improve model generalization by penalizing large coefficients.

To evaluate model performance, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) was computed for each model on the test dataset. This allowed for a quantitative comparison of predictive accuracy across the different approaches. By comparing these models, the study aims to determine which method provides the best balance between interpretability and predictive performance.

Exploratory data analysis

Table 1 presents the summary statistics for all variables in the training set. The response variable mdev ranges from $5,000 to $50,000 with a mean of $22,760, reflecting moderate variability in home values across suburbs. The predictor crim is notably right-skewed, with a median of 0.23 and a maximum of 88.98, indicating the presence of high-crime outlier suburbs. Similarly, zn has a median of zero, suggesting most suburbs have no large residential lots zoned. The variable age has a mean of 67.87, implying that the majority of suburbs consist of older housing stock. The binary variable chas has a mean of 0.07, confirming that only approximately 7% of suburbs border the Charles River. The summary statistics reveal considerable variation across predictors, with evidence of skewness in several variables that may warrant attention during modelling.

The distributions of all 11 quantitative variables in the training set are shown in Figure 1. Several variables exhibit strong right skewness, most notably crim, zn, and lstat, where the majority of observations are concentrated at low values with long tails extending rightward indicating the presence of outlier suburbs with very high crime rates, large zoned lots, and high proportions of lower-status residents respectively.

The variable tax shows a bimodal distribution with two distinct clusters, suggesting two groups of suburbs with markedly different tax rate regimes. Similarly, patratio is left-skewed with most suburbs concentrated at higher pupil-teacher ratios around 20. The variables rm and medv approximate a roughly normal distribution, while age and nox are moderately left-skewed, indicating that older properties and higher pollution levels are more prevalent across suburbs. The variable dis is right-skewed, with most suburbs located relatively close to employment centres.

Figure 2 shows that chas is highly imbalanced with approximately 93% of suburbs not bordering the Charles River. For rad, suburbs cluster at indices 4, 5, and 24, with the spike at index 24 suggesting a distinct group of suburbs with exceptionally high highway accessibility.

Linear regression model

The full model explains approximately 72% of the variance in median home values (R² = 0.7292, Adjusted R² = 0.7208), with a Residual Standard Error of 4.829 and a highly significant F-statistic of 86.85 on 12 variables and 387 degrees of freedom (p < 2.2e-16). As shown in ?@tbl-fullmodel, most predictors are statistically significant at the 5% level. The variables rm (β = 3.725) and lstat (β = −0.562) are the strongest predictors, consistent with the earlier correlation analysis. However, indus (p = 0.538) and age (p = 0.855) are not statistically significant, suggesting that they add little new information beyond other predictors, this highly supports variable selection for better model performance.

Interaction Term

The best model identified in the previous section the AIC Stepwise model was extended by incorporating an interaction term between average number of rooms (rm) and Lower status of the population (lstat) (rm × lstat). This interaction is motivated by the observation that the effect of the number of rooms on median home value is not constant across all neighbourhoods, but rather depends on the socioeconomic status of the neighbourhood (lstat). Specifically, an additional room in a wealthy neighbourhood (low lstat) is expected to add significantly more value to a home than an additional room in a poorer neighbourhood (high lstat). This is supported by the strong individual correlations of both rm (r= 0.69) and lstat (r = −0.74) with medv observed in the correlation analysis, suggesting a meaningful joint effect worth capturing. The interaction term rm × lstat therefore allows the slope of rm on medv to vary as a function of lstat, producing a more flexible and realistic model.

The Residuals vs Fitted plot shows points randomly scattered around zero with the orange smooth line remaining close to the horizontal, indicating that the linearity assumption is reasonably satisfied. However, a slight pattern is visible at higher fitted values, suggesting mild non-linearity at the upper end of predicted home values.

The Normal Q-Q plot shows that residuals follow the diagonal line reasonably well in the middle range, confirming approximate normality. However, both tails deviate from the line particularly the upper tail indicating the presence of heavy tails and some extreme residuals. The histogram of residuals supports this, showing an approximately bell-shaped distribution centred at zero but with a slight right skew and a few large positive residuals, suggesting mild departure from perfect normality.

The Scale-Location plot reveals a slightly upward trending red line, indicating that the spread of residuals increases with fitted values. This suggests mild heteroscedasticity the variance of residuals is not perfectly constant across all fitted values, which is a minor violation of the homoscedasticity assumption. The assumptions of linearity and normality are reasonably satisfied, while mild heteroscedasticity is present. These violations are not severe enough to invalidate the model but suggest that a variance-stabilising transformation such as a log transformation of medv could further improve model performance.

Conclusion

This analysis set out to predict the median value of owner-occupied homes across Boston suburbs using multiple linear regression and regularization techniques. The AIC Stepwise model with an rm x lstat interaction term emerged as the best performing model, achieving a Test MSE of 13.5784 — a substantial improvement over all additive models.

Key Predictors of Median Home Value

The analysis consistently identified (lstat) and (rm) as the most influential predictors across all models. Higher proportions of lower-status residents are strongly associated with lower home values, reflecting the well-established relationship between neighborhood socioeconomic status and property values. Conversely, a greater average number of rooms is associated with higher home values, capturing the effect of housing size on market value. Additional factors that negatively impact home values include high air pollution , greater distance from employment centers , higher pupil-teacher ratios , higher crime rates , and higher tax rates . Proximity to the Charles River (chas) and better highway accessibility (rad) positively contribute to home values. The variables indus and age were found to be statistically insignificant and were removed by both AIC and BIC stepwise selection, suggesting that industrial activity and housing age add little predictive value beyond what is already captured by the remaining predictors.

Moreover the inclusion of the interaction term reduced the Test MSE from 22.5276 to 13.5784 and increased R² from 0.7289 to 0.7895, representing the single largest performance improvement across all models. This confirms that the value added by an additional room is significantly greater in wealthy neighborhoods (low lstat) than in poorer ones, and that room size and neighborhood socioeconomic status jointly determine home values rather than acting independently.
